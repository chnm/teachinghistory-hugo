### Textbook Silences

In many U.S. history textbooks, Black Americans are largely written out of the story of the American Revolution. Individual figures such as Crispus Attucks — a real person considered to be the first colonist killed during the Boston Massacre — appear, but beyond such brief mentions textbook narratives tend to offer little sustained attention to the roles Black Americans played during the Revolutionary era.

In many textbooks, Black Americans reenter the narrative only at the end of the story, in discussions of emancipation in the northern states or the long-term consequences of the Revolution for slavery. While these are important developments, this framing reinforces the idea that Black Americans were peripheral to the Revolution itself and rather than active participants throughout the unfolding of the crisis.

This silence on Black roles during the Revolution reflects the same structural pressures that shape textbook narratives more broadly. With the competing interests of limited space and expansive coverage, textbooks often present the Revolution as a relatively straightforward story about liberty, individual rights, and the emergence of a new nation. Complexity is often minimized. As a result, Black Americans, if included at all, are frequently treated as exceptions to the narrative rather than as integral to it.

### Historians Focus on Agency

Yet historical scholarship tells a very different story: Black Americans played significant and varied roles throughout the Revolutionary era. Thousands of Black Americans fought on both sides of the conflict. Many thousands of others decided to remain neutral. Each of these individuals made strategic decisions oftentimes in pursuit of their own freedom and thus shaped the meaning and consequences of the Revolution itself. Far from being marginal figures, they were central actors in a deeply contested struggle over liberty.

Historians have long worked to recover and interpret these experiences. In 1961, historian Benjamin Quarles offered a powerful reframing of Black participation in the Revolution. “[Black Americans]role in the Revolution,” wrote Quarles,” can best be understood by realizing that his major loyalty was not to a place nor to a people but to a principle. Insofar as he had freedom of choice, he was likely to join the side that made him the quickest and best offer in terms of those ‘inalienable rights’ of which Mr. Jefferson had spoken.” 

This emphasis on choice highlights a key concept in historical thinking: agency. Black Americans were not passive recipients of the Revolution’s outcomes; they actively shaped their own paths within the constraints they faced. For many, the war presented both risks and opportunities—a chance, however uncertain, to move closer to freedom.

Later historians have further complicated this picture. Gary Nash, for example, describes Black Americans as fighting “a revolution within the Revolution.” 

Nash writes:

> In the centuries-long history of Africans in America, the struggle to gain freedom and wrest equality from a resistant white society has been the consuming desire that has kept harrowed bodies and weary souls going. In this struggle to cross the river from bondage to freedom, the American Revolution had enormous importance. It marked the first mass slave rebellion in American history, initiated the first civil rights movement, spawned the first large-scale constructions of free black life, brought forth the first written testimonies from African Americans who wanted the world to hear of their strivings and freedom claims, involved the first emergence of what W.E.B. Du Bois called the ‘talented tenth,’ and had international repercussions.

One of the most striking aspects of Black participation in the Revolutionary War is the extent to which it disrupted later assumptions about race and military service. As Nash points out, the Revolutionary War was the last American conflict until the mid-twentieth century to feature integrated fighting units. In the North, Black soldiers served alongside white soldiers, often at higher rates relative to their population and on average for longer enlistments. In the decades that followed the American Revolution, Black abolitionists would frequently cite their Revolutionary military service as evidence that they were deserving of freedom and citizenship. 

While over 5,000 Black Americans joined the Patriot cause both as soldiers and as [camp followers](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/camp-followers) (which included many women), a significant number of Black men and women chose a different path: they joined the British. This decision was likely not rooted in loyalty to the Crown, but in a desire to seek their own liberty. In 1775, Lord Dunmore, colonial governor of Virginia, issued a proclamation promising freedom to enslaved people who escaped from rebel enslavers and joined British forces. The response was immediate and substantial. Thousands of enslaved men, women, and children fled to British lines, seeking to secure their emancipation through military service.

Importantly, historians have shown that this movement toward British forces was not simply a reaction to Dunmore’s proclamation. Even before it was issued, enslaved people were already escaping and attempting to negotiate their freedom. The proclamation formalized and expanded an existing pattern of resistance, underscoring once again the agency of Black Americans in shaping their own destinies.

### Complexity in the Classroom

For teachers, incorporating these perspectives into the classroom requires allowing students the time and space to wrestle with competing narratives and conflicting evidence. One effective approach is to foreground primary sources that reveal the presence and participation of Black Americans during the Revolution. The Declaration of Independence itself offers an entry point. Among its grievances against the British Crown is the accusation that the king incited "domestic insurrections”—a reference, in part, to efforts to encourage enslaved people to seek their freedom. This language can open up nuanced discussions about how the Revolution connected to themes of liberty and independence in different ways to different groups.

Individual stories can also be powerful teaching tools. Figures such as Primus Hall—a Black soldier who later petitioned for rights and recognition—provide concrete examples of how Black Americans participated in the war and continued to advocate for freedom afterward. [Women such as Belinda Sutton](https://royallhouse.org/slavery/belinda-sutton-and-her-petitions/) also directly advocated for their own freedom by petitioning the government in this period and these petitions can be effectively used in the classroom. Pension records, petitions, and military documents can help students trace these experiences and understand the contributions that are often left out of textbook narratives.

Visual sources, such as a print of Black soldiers from the 1st Rhode Island Regiment, can further reinforce the crucial participation of Black Americans. These sources invite students to ask deeper questions about why certain stories have been emphasized while others have been marginalized.

Another valuable strategy is to engage students in historical interpretation. For example, teachers can have students examine such sources as Dunmore’s Proclamation and learn about the thousands of enslaved people who joined the Loyalists and British to fight for their own freedom. From there students might be tasked with coming up with a title for an exhibit or textbook chapter about the American Revolution that encompasses a more inclusive range of historical perspectives. Activities such as these push students beyond simple answers and into the kind of complex reasoning that defines historical thinking.

Introducing Dunmore’s Proclamation to students also provides an excellent opportunity to discuss the role of women and children who also took the opportunities to escape to British lines during the Revolution. As this [post from Colonial Williamsburg](https://www.colonialwilliamsburg.org/discover/18th-century-people/stories-of-women/escaping-to-freedom/) makes clear, Black women like Nanny, Eve, Aggy, and Lucy also made strategic decisions about freedom. Taking advantage of the proximity of British forces in Tidewater Virginia in 1781 these four enslaved women fled the estate of Peyton Randolph.     

Using sources like those above to support student inquiry aligns with what education scholar Brenda Santos describes as the distinction between “figuring out” and “finding out” questions. Rather than treating history as a set of fixed facts to be memorized, it encourages students to grapple with ambiguity, weigh evidence, and construct interpretations. In this context, the American Revolution becomes not a single, unified story, but a collection of overlapping and sometimes conflicting narratives.

Teachers can take this a step further by asking students to revise textbook accounts themselves. What would it look like to write a history of the Revolution that fully incorporates Black Americans’ experiences? How can multiple perspectives be included without collapsing into confusion? These are challenging questions, but they invite students to engage directly with the process of historical construction.

Ultimately, the goal is not simply to add more content, but to rethink the framework of the narrative itself. Students should be encouraged to ask: what part of the story is missing and where can we find it? The American Revolution was not driven solely by a unified group of colonial leaders, nor did it produce a single, shared outcome. It was a complex and contested process in which many different groups—including Black Americans—pursued their own visions of freedom.

By bringing these perspectives into the classroom, teachers can offer students a richer and more accurate understanding of the past. Just as importantly, they can model a form of historical inquiry that values complexity, acknowledges multiple viewpoints, and recognizes the agency of all participants. In doing so, we move closer to a history that reflects not only what happened, but how it was experienced by the many people who lived it.
